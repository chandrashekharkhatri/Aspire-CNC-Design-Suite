## 🚀 A Guided Setup Walkthrough

After the wizard finishes and the shortcut appears in your Start menu, your first launch matters more than you might think.

**Step 1 — Choose your workspace units.** You will be asked whether you prefer inches or millimeters. Pick the one your machine and materials speak. Mixing units mid-project is the single most common source of "why is this tiny" moments.

**Step 2 — Let the application detect your machine profile.** If your CNC controller is a common one, the software can preload a post-processor so exported toolpaths match your hardware. If you do not see yours, choose the closest generic option and refine later.

**Step 3 — Set your material defaults.** Enter the sheet size you actually buy. Most sign shops work in standard 4x8 sheets, but hobbyists often use smaller blanks. Telling the software your real stock size prevents layout surprises.

**Step 4 — Calibrate your preview.** Open a sample project and run the simulation. Watch how the virtual tool moves. This is your rehearsal, and rehearsals are where confidence is built.

**Step 5 — Save a template.** Once your units, machine, and material defaults feel right, save the whole configuration as a template. Future projects then start from a sensible baseline instead of a blank slate.

## 🔍 Feature Deep Dive

Let us unpack the headline features with more texture, because bullet lists only scratch the surface.

### 🎨 Vector-First Design

Everything in this environment begins as a vector. Lines, arcs, and curves define the boundaries of what will be cut. The node-editing tools are powerful enough for intricate scrollwork yet approachable enough that a first-timer can clean up a traced logo in an afternoon. Snapping, mirroring, and array duplication make repetitive ornamentation painless.

### 🏔 Three-Dimensional Relief Modeling

Relief carving is where the software truly shines. You can start from a flat vector boundary and sculpt a raised or recessed surface with brushes that add, subtract, smooth, and blend. The metaphor is clay: push material up, pull it down, feather the edges until light rolls across the surface the way it would across a real carving.

### 🧱 Component Assembly

Complex carvings are rarely a single shape. They are assemblies — a central medallion, a frame, some flourishes, a background texture. The component system lets you treat each of these as a distinct layer, position them in three-dimensional space, and then bake them into one unified relief for toolpathing.

### 🧮 Toolpath Intelligence

Toolpath generation is the bridge between a pretty model and a real cut. The software offers strategies for every common need:

- **Roughing passes** that hog out material quickly with a large bit.
- **Finishing passes** that trace fine detail with a small or tapered tool.
- **V-carving** that produces crisp lettering with variable width.
- **Pocketing** that clears interiors cleanly.
- **Profiling** that cuts parts free from the sheet.

Each strategy exposes parameters like stepover, feed rate, and plunge rate. Learning to tune these is the difference between a fifteen-minute cut and a forty-minute one that still needs sanding.

### 📚 Material and Tool Libraries

Nobody wants to re-enter the same bit dimensions every single session. Libraries let you save tool geometry and material properties once, then reuse them forever. This consistency is what separates a hobby that feels chaotic from a craft that feels controlled.

### 🎥 Simulation and Preview

Before a single chip flies, you can watch a virtual cutter carve your design. The simulation catches collisions, impossibly deep cuts, and forgotten clearances. It is cheap insurance against expensive mistakes.

## 🔧 Working With Toolpaths

Toolpaths deserve their own section because they are where beginners stumble most.

A toolpath is a set of instructions that tells the machine where to move, how fast, and how deep. When you generate one, you are translating a three-dimensional model into a sequence of linear and arc movements the controller can execute.

The golden rule: **never cut a new material with a new toolpath without simulating first.** Simulation is free. Broken bits and ruined stock are not.

The second golden rule: **start conservative.** Reduce feed rates and depth of cut for your first pass on any unfamiliar material. You can always speed up once you see how the machine behaves. You cannot un-break a snapped carbide bit.

For those pursuing precision, keep a logbook. Write down the material, the bit, the feeds, the speeds, and the result. Over months, this log becomes more valuable than any forum post, because it reflects your machine, your climate, and your technique.

## 🖼 Vector Art, Reliefs, and 3D Components

The creative library bundled with this environment is generous, but the real magic happens when you import your own artwork.

- **Tracing** converts bitmap logos into editable vectors. Clean source images produce clean traces; blurry sources produce messy ones. Garbage in, garbage out.
- **Relief imports** let you bring in three-dimensional models and incorporate them into a composition.
- **Texturing tools** apply procedural patterns — wood grain, stucco, woven fabric — that add depth without hand-sculpting every nuance.

Treat imported art as raw clay, not finished marble. Clean nodes, close open paths, and simplify before you cut. Your machine will thank you with a smoother finish.

## 🩺 Troubleshooting Common Hiccups

**The software launches but the 3D view is black.**
Update your graphics driver. If that fails, check whether the application is using the integrated GPU instead of a dedicated one, and force it to use the discrete card.

**Toolpaths take forever to calculate.**
Large models with tiny stepovers are computationally heavy. Increase the stepover slightly, or reduce the model resolution where fine detail is not needed.

**The simulation looks wrong compared to the preview.**
Confirm your stock thickness and zero position are set correctly. A mismatch here is the usual culprit.

**Exports are rejected by my controller.**
You may have selected the wrong post-processor. Switch to the profile that matches your machine's brand and firmware generation.

**The interface looks cramped on my laptop.**
Enable the compact panel mode and adjust Windows display scaling. The responsive layout handles most cases, but manual nudges help on very small screens.

## ⚡ Performance Tuning Tips

A few habits keep the environment snappy even on modest hardware:

- Close browser tabs while simulating heavy models; memory is shared.
- Keep your project on a solid-state drive rather than an external spinning disk.
- Reduce undo history length for very large files.
- Hide unused components rather than deleting them if you may need them later.
- Restart the application between marathon sessions to clear memory caches.

These small rituals compound. A workstation that feels fast keeps you in flow, and flow is where good work happens.
